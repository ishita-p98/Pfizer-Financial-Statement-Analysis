# 02 — Business environment and competitive economics

## Business model
Pfizer develops, manufactures and commercializes medicines and vaccines across oncology, vaccines, inflammation/immunology, internal medicine, rare diseases and hospital products. The report emphasizes the combination of internal R&D, licensing, manufacturing scale, alliances and acquisitions.

## Industry economics
Biopharmaceutical economics combine high upfront R&D expense and regulatory uncertainty with patent-protected cash flows for successful products. Expiring exclusivity, generic/biosimilar entry and payer negotiations can rapidly erode individual product economics. This makes **pipeline replacement** and acquisition discipline important.

## Competitive framework
The report applies Porter’s Five Forces: low threat of entry because of regulatory, manufacturing and research barriers; low-to-moderate supplier bargaining power; moderate buyer power from insurers, governments and pharmacy benefit managers; moderate substitution threat; and high incumbent rivalry.

These qualitative labels are the group's assessments, not independently measured industry scores.

## Selected peers
- **Merck:** oncology and vaccine franchises, including Keytruda and Gardasil.
- **Eli Lilly:** metabolic and obesity treatments, with expanding research exposure elsewhere.
- **AbbVie:** immunology franchises and transition after Humira loss of exclusivity.

## Strategic tension
The report describes Seagen, Biohaven and Global Blood Therapeutics as external portfolio investments. Seagen especially increases oncology exposure but brings debt financing and acquisition-related intangible assets. The relevant question is not simply whether acquired revenue grows, but whether **risk-adjusted returns on acquired assets exceed the cost of capital**.

## What the financial statements reveal
A high goodwill/intangibles share is consistent with acquisition-led expansion, but it also places weight on management forecasts for future product cash flows. R&D spending as a percentage of sales is informative yet not a standalone measure of research productivity, especially across firms with different acquisition and accounting strategies.
